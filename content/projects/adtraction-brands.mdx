## A partner shopping for a brand

Adtraction is a Nordic affiliate network. Partners are publishers, influencers and comparison sites, and they log in to find a brand to promote, read its terms and grab a tracking link. The brands directory is the front door to that whole business.

The reader of this page is a partner with an audience and an evening: a beauty blogger, say, looking for something worth writing about this month. What they are doing is closer to shopping than to querying a database. They want to see what a brand sells, whether it fits their readers, what it pays, and whether anything is going on right now, such as a promo code or a commission increase. For years the platform answered them with a table.

This case study is mostly about visual design: hierarchy, imagery, type and what a partner sees first. The old and new platforms ran side by side for months, so the result is measured, not guessed.

## What was there, and why it did not work

The old platform was not broken. Every fact a partner needed was on it somewhere. The problem was that it was organised the way the data was stored, not the way a partner decides.

### Every brand weighed the same

Pick a category from the sidebar and you got a table: a logo, the name as a blue link, a one-line pitch in Swedish ("FANTASTISKA RABATTER. PÅ ALLT. ALLTID."), two square badges marked F and P under columns with no header, then EPC and commission. Every row was the same height and the same weight. A brand that paid 10% and had a campaign running looked exactly like one with no earnings history at all ("No data").

The [law of similarity](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-similarity/) says that things which look alike are read as the same kind of thing. That suits a price list. It does not suit a page whose job is to tell a partner which clothing brand in the list is worth their evening.

### It showed the logo, not what the brand sells

The only picture in a row was the logo, and a logo says who a brand is, not what it sells or who buys it. A wordmark in black capitals tells a beauty blogger nothing about whether the products would look right on their feed. [Nielsen Norman Group's eyetracking research on web photos](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/) found that people study images that carry information, such as product photos, and skip images that are only decoration. The brands already had that information: every program uploads campaign images. The table just never showed them.

### The brand page had everything and ranked nothing

Open a brand and the page started with a greeting ("Hej Albert DK"), then a channel dropdown and an "Apply to advertiser" button, then tabs of presentation text in the brand's own words. The commission table and a list of policies sat in a column down the right. There were no performance numbers on the page at all; to see whether a brand actually converted, a partner had to leave for a report.

The rules for promo codes, emails and brand strategy were long and legally important, and they filled the main column, so they were the first thing a partner read. [Nielsen's research on how people read the web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/) found that visitors have time to read at most 28% of the words on a page, and about 20% is more likely. On the old page that budget went on terms before the partner knew whether the brand was worth it.

## What I did: a storefront

I worked from the Laws of UX and Nielsen Norman Group's research, and from one test for every element: does it help a partner decide, or act?

**Make it look like a shop.** Brands sit in horizontal rows a partner scans sideways: For you, New brands, Promo codes, Promotions, Commission increase. The sidebar list of categories became a strip of outline icons with their names under them across the top, which uses the width a dashboard has and keeps the filter chips on one line. [Jakob's law](https://lawsofux.com/jakobs-law/) says users spend most of their time on other sites and expect yours to work the same way. Partners spend their days in shops and streaming apps, and rows of image cards are the pattern they already know. The table still exists behind "View all" and the list view for anyone who wants it, but it is no longer the first thing you meet.

**Give every brand a picture.** Each card carries the brand's campaign image with the logo on a white plate over it. The plate does work a table row could not: a black wordmark and a pastel logo sit at the same visual weight because the plate normalises what is behind them. Brands that only supplied a logo still get a card that reads as a card.

**Make the commission the headline.** On each card the number is set large and the unit small: "10% per sale", "40.13 EUR per lead". The EPC sits in a quiet grey pill beside it, and a brand with no history says "No EPC yet" instead of showing a dash. The old table gave EPC and commission the same weight, so partners read whichever came first. Status tags (New, For You, Commission Increase) each have their own colour and icon, pinned to the top-left of the image where the eye lands.

![Further down: Promo codes, Promotions and Commission increase rows, each card with its own campaign photograph](/projects/adtraction-brands/new-brands-scrolled.jpg)

**Put the decision on the brand page.** The page opens on the same campaign image and logo plate the partner just clicked, so it feels like opening the card rather than loading a different page. Under it, chips state what is on offer: Fair Tracking, Commission Increase, Promo Code, Promotions, Product Feed and the category. Then three numbers in the largest type on the page after the brand name: earnings per click, conversion rate and average order value for the last 60 days. Those are the three a partner uses to decide, and they used to live in a separate report. Coffee Friend runs in six markets, which used to be six programs to find one by one; now the sister markets sit under the title as flag-and-name links, so a Danish partner who landed on the Swedish page is one click from the right one.

**Fold the rules, keep the count.** Brand-specific promotional rules sit behind "View full description", with a badge showing how many there are (seven, for Coffee Friend) and the line "Brand-specific promotional rules to review". NN/g's article on [progressive disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/) says to show only a few of the most important options first, and that whatever appears on the first display tells users it is important. The rules matter, but they matter after the partner has decided the brand is worth it. The count keeps them from being hidden.

**Make the tracking link the thing that stands out.** Everything a partner needs to start promoting is in one card on the right: logo download, channel selector, and the tracking link in a filled green pill with a copy button, the only full-width filled control in that card. The [Von Restorff effect](https://lawsofux.com/von-restorff-effect/) says the item that differs is the one people notice and remember. On a page full of chips and numbers, the link is what the partner came for, so it is the one thing styled to be different.

**Build it once, in the toolkit.** None of this is styled one page at a time. The chips, pills, cards, filter buttons and outline icon set are components in the shared Adtraction UI toolkit, which I also work on. The image plate, the commission typography and the tag colours were designed in Figma as reusable patterns first and then built as components, so the same card renders on the dashboard and on the brands page, and a change to it lands everywhere at once. On the brands micro-frontend, 154 of the 260 commits on the partner-facing brand pages are mine, covering the brand page structure, the header and policy sections, the category filters and the sort logic. Colleagues built the micro-frontend composition and later added the "For you" row.

The same storefront idea runs through the features overview, where each platform capability is a card in a bento grid sized by importance. Features that are not available yet stay in the grid at reduced contrast, so the page also works as a map of what is coming.

## Measured against the old platform

Adtraction runs Microsoft Clarity on both platforms, and both are live at the same time: some partners have moved to the new platform and some have not. That makes for an unusually clean comparison. Same 90 days, same product, two interfaces.

These are session-level numbers for sessions that visited the relevant pages, last 90 days to 3 September 2026.

| Brand page | Old platform | New platform |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sessions | 15,019 | 58,873 |
| Quick backs | 68.0% | 28.9% |
| Dead clicks | 21.7% | 17.4% |
| Rage clicks | 0.23% | 0.62% |
| Pages per session | 8.1 | 6.7 |
| Active time per session | 3.6 min | 3.0 min |

| Brands directory and brand pages together | Old platform | New platform |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sessions | 17,220 | 100,236 |
| Quick backs | 67.1% | 22.7% |
| Dead clicks | 21.3% | 12.7% |
| Rage clicks | 0.20% | 0.42% |
| Pages per session | 7.7 | 4.9 |
| Active time per session | 3.4 min | 2.1 min |

Quick backs are the number I watch. Clarity counts one when a user opens a page and leaves it almost immediately, which on a brand page means "this was not what I wanted". Two thirds of old brand-page sessions had at least one. On the new page it is under a third. The card now shows the partner what they are about to open, so fewer of them open the wrong thing. Dead clicks (clicks that did nothing) fell too, which is what you would expect when the whole card is the link instead of one blue name in a table.

Fewer pages and less time per session read as wins to me on a discovery surface: the partner found the brand and the link in fewer hops. I would not claim that without the quick-back number next to it, because on its own "less time" could mean either.

**Where it went the wrong way.** Rage clicks roughly tripled on the brand page, from 0.23% to 0.62%. Still small, but a real regression and I am not hiding it. I have not yet gone through the recordings to find where, but the two places the new design invites a click and answers slowly are the copy button on the tracking link and the folded description. That is where I would look first.

**Caveats a reader should have.** These are sessions, not page views, so a session counts once no matter how many brand pages it opened. The two groups are different partners, not the same partners before and after, and partners who opted into the new platform early may be more engaged than average. The gap is large enough that I do not think any of that explains it, but it is a comparison, not an experiment.

### What partners said

Clarity tells you what people did, not what they thought. The feedback address the rollout ran on caught some of the second part.

A German finance partner, three weeks after launch:

> The design feels cleaner, and the individual elements (Pulse Check, Account Balance, Advertiser selection) are visually better separated. The sidebar categories are also easier to understand and more organized.

The same email said the brand selection felt cluttered, because the same brand showed up once per country. That was fair, and it has since been rebuilt. A partner in Spain, who had evaluated several competing networks while onboarding:

> I've been looking around, and honestly, your platform is undoubtedly the best on the market, by far. And I've studied several.

Half a dozen partners wrote in unprompted with something positive during the rollout. That is not a survey and I am not going to treat it as one: people who email a feedback address are self-selected, and the partners who disliked the new platform mostly just switched back instead of writing. It is the qualitative half next to the quick-back number, and nothing more than that.

## What I would do next

- Fix the rage-click hotspots on the brand page: give the copy button an immediate state change, and open the description inline instead of in a modal.
- Bring the three headline metrics onto the card, at least for the "For you" row, so the decision can happen before the click.
- The new page scrolls to 99% depth on average, which tells me the rows below the fold are being read but also that the page may be too long. Test cutting one row.
