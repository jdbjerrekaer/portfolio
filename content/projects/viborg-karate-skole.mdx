## Three versions of one club

Viborg Karate Skole has trained in Viborg since 9 May 1975, and in 1987 it became the first place in Europe to teach Ashihara karate. Its website has existed in three versions:

1. **The club's own WordPress site.** Old, plain and honest. A recruitment poster as the header, a sidebar menu of 36 links, and 177 posts of tournament reports, gallery years and newspaper clippings.
2. **The agency version.** A modern single-page app delivered by an agency. It looked finished. It also used a stock photo of muay thai as the hero of a karate club, and most of its sentences existed nowhere in the club's own material.
3. **The rebuild.** Mine. A static site that keeps the agency's colours and fonts, and puts the club's own history, photos and wording back in.

Before touching anything I archived the first two: every route of the agency site at desktop and phone widths, and the WordPress pages with their text. The comparisons below come from those captures.

## What the agency shipped, and why it did not work

The agency version was generated with an AI coding agent and shipped close to its defaults. Nothing in it was broken, which is what made it hard to argue with. Every part of it was competent, and every part of it could have belonged to any club in the country. So I went through it section by section and measured it instead of describing it.

### It sold a different sport

The first thing a parent saw was a stock photo of muay thai: gloves, pads and thai shorts, on the home page of a karate club that has a 50-year photo archive. Nielsen Norman Group's eyetracking work on photos found that people look closely at pictures of real people and real places, and skip images that look like decoration. A stock photo is not neutral filler. It is the first evidence a visitor gets about who runs the place, and here it was evidence of someone who had never been in the dojo.

### Every section was the same section

Eyebrow label, centred all-caps heading, a row of identical cards, a button. The "Find dit hold" section is six cards with the same layout, the same badges and the same two buttons each, so a parent has to read all six to find the one that fits a seven-year-old.

The [law of similarity](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-similarity/) says that things which look alike are read as the same kind of thing. That is right for a list of equals and wrong for a page whose whole job is to tell a toddler class from an adult one. Repeating one card style six times also cancels out the [Von Restorff effect](https://lawsofux.com/von-restorff-effect/): when every element is styled for attention, none of them gets it. Two buttons of equal weight on every card, "Læs om holdet" and "Prøv gratis", doubled the choices at every step, which is the cost [Hick's law](https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/) describes.

### The words were nobody's

I wrote a script that compares every sentence on the agency site with the club's own WordPress text. 154 sentences existed only in the agency version. They were the sentences a language model writes when it has no facts: "stærkt fællesskab", six-item lists of virtues, and questions instead of headings.

Nielsen's research on how people read the web found that visitors read at most about a quarter of the words on a page and scan for the rest. Generic copy wastes that small budget. "Fællesskab, Selvtillid, Fokus & koncentration, Alsidig motion, Disciplin & respekt, Personlig udvikling" is true of every sport, so a scanning parent learns nothing that would make them pick this club.

The voices section was the clearest case. Every testimonial was a template: a name, then bold labels such as "Givende oplevelse:" and "Rolle:", filled with one smooth sentence each.

### The facts had drifted

The street number was 98, not 98A. The phone number was not the one the club uses now. The history said the club was "one of the first" in Europe to teach Ashihara karate, when it was the first. The year the dojo opened in Viborg Hallen was a year off. And the "Arrangementer" section advertised summer camps and events that no longer exist. None of these errors look wrong, and each one is the kind a parent finds out about at the door.

### Why it mattered

Stanford's web credibility research found that the look of a site was the most common thing people mentioned when they judged whether to trust it, ahead of the content. A template is not neutral either. Visitors have seen this exact layout on dozens of other sites, so it tells them the club bought a website rather than made one, and that impression arrives before they have read a word.

## What I did: gave it a personality

The goal was a site that could only belong to this club. Everything in it should come from the dojo, the archive or the people, and nothing from a template.

**A direction with a name.** I called it "dojo floor": flat, bold and physical, like mats and a painted wall. The owner liked the colours and the logo, so the red, navy, Oswald and Plus Jakarta Sans stayed. The template styling went: solid colour slabs instead of gradients, hard offset shadows instead of blur, left-aligned headings, one primary button per area.

**The club's own symbols as design.** The war cry "WE, NOT ME" is set huge in red and white because the club actually shouts it. The timeline years use Chow Fun, the lettering from the club's own documents. The timetable is coloured in belt order. The 1975 recruitment poster sits tilted with a hard shadow, like a flyer pinned to the dojo wall.

**The club's words and photos back.** Every agency-only sentence was rewritten from the club's own text or replaced with it, taking the count from 154 to 51, and each of the 51 is kept on purpose with its reason written down. Every photo is the club's own, from its archive. No quote is invented, and no photo is attached to a person unless I could verify it was them.

**Reading pages built for the job.** The children's syllabus was a grid of cards. Now a belt navigator sticks to the top of the page, shows the belt you are reading about and links to the previous and next one, so a child can see where they are going.

**Every old link kept alive.** The WordPress site had 272 public URLs and the agency site had its own routes. Over 600 redirects send them to the closest page in the new site, so a link in a 2014 newspaper article still lands somewhere useful.

**Cheap to own.** A Next.js static export built for Cloudflare Pages' free plan: no CMS, no server, no monthly hosting bill. The contact form posts to a form service with an invisible captcha. Analytics only load after a visitor says yes in a cookie banner in the site's own design and plain Danish, with "Nej tak" as easy to press as "Ja tak".

## Working with the owner

The owner is not a web person, and the decisions that mattered were the owner's. I kept a running list of open questions in the repo and asked them in batches: is this photo the club's, which belt colour comes after red, is the phone number still right. Several of the best fixes came straight from those answers, including "first in Europe", which the owner corrected with a newspaper article as the source.

When the owner asked for changes after the first version, I tagged the state before and after, so each request is a diff to look at rather than a promise.

## Status

The rebuild runs on a preview server and is ready to move to sabaki.dk once the DNS is moved. The before-state is archived, so the comparison stays checkable after the old sites are gone.
