*University work. It is here because the method is still how I work.*

## The result

BiomBrane is a tangible 3D plant-cell model for first-year high-school biology, paired with a web interface. It replaces a pen-and-paper exercise with a physical object students manipulate, and gives hints when a group gets stuck so they are not sitting idle waiting for the one teacher in the room.

We took it from field observation in a real classroom through to a prototype we evaluated with that same class. IT Product Development at Aarhus University, 2019, four of us on it.

## My role

The concept was mine and I led the project. With four people and a fixed term, most of my time went into planning, dividing the work, and keeping the thread between the field study and what we actually built — making sure the prototype answered the problem we had observed rather than the one that was most fun to build. I worked across the research, the design and the build rather than owning a single part.

This was my first larger project, and the first time I ran a full product arc rather than a piece of one.

## We framed the problem from observation, not assumption

We started with ethnographic observation in a real high-school biology class, using contextual-design methods, and built work models and scenarios from what we actually saw rather than what we expected.

What the field study told us: biology is theory-heavy, live-specimen experiments carry ethical objections, teacher prep time is the bottleneck, and students lose learning time queuing for help from the one adult in the room. That last one became the thing we designed against.

We grounded the design in learning-science research and mapped it to the Danish Ministry of Education curriculum goals, so the tool taught what the class was actually assessed on rather than what we found interesting.

## We tested it with the class we had watched

Groups of two, one group of four, and a whole-class demonstration. Criteria set in advance: ease of use, learning value, social activity. We limited our own intervention to moments when students were genuinely stuck, so the results reflected the tool rather than us hovering. The exercise was photosynthesis, matched to their upcoming exam.

Students collaborated in focused groups and talked about the tasks — a visible change from the passive behaviour we had watched on our first visit.

**Dyslexic students benefited most** from the tangible interaction. That was an accessibility outcome we had designed for, not one we stumbled into.

## We costed two routes to market

Selling outright to schools through procurement, and renting.

We argued for renting. The plant cell is a small slice of the curriculum, so buying it outright is too large an investment per student reached — which is exactly the objection a school would raise in the room. A rental model answers it, and turns the size of the product from a weakness into a non-issue.

## What we would do next

Too few tasks for real curriculum depth. And we had pushed the hardware close to fully exploited, so the next increment of depth would have had to come from the software rather than the object — which changes what the product is, and is worth deciding deliberately rather than drifting into.
