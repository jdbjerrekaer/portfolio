## What this case study is about

The redesign is the occasion. The subject is how I work.

An autonomous agent running on my own hardware built the entire first version of this concept while I was away from the keyboard. When I came back, I reviewed it the way I would review a junior designer's work, and that review ran about thirty rounds across a day.

That gap is the interesting part. A capable agent, given a researched brief, produced something coherent that was still wrong in roughly thirty specific ways — and almost none of those ways were things the brief had mentioned. They were things I only knew when I saw them. Working out *why* I only knew them then, and not before, is what this piece is about.

## The client situation

A Danish pest-control business runs two separate websites. One is a webshop selling DIY products: traps, baits, sprays. The other sells professional treatment, where a technician comes to your house.

A visitor has to already know which of those they need before they land on either. Neither site says: same company, two options, here is how to tell which one you are.

## Auditing the existing shop

I audited this as a webshop, not as a homepage. I walked the real purchase path at desktop and phone widths, put a product in the basket, went to checkout, and separately measured the craft of the interface itself. Baymard's e-commerce guidance is the reference for the funnel because it is built on observed purchase sessions rather than opinion.

Before any of it, the question a client is entitled to ask: **does this need a redesign, or does it need a few tickets?** Most audit findings are the second thing. Sorting by that test is what shaped this section, and it changed which finding leads.

**Credit where it is due.** Category pages have working faceted filters and a sort control. Checkout allows guest purchase, which plenty of Danish shops still get wrong. Payment covers Apple Pay, MobilePay, card and EAN invoicing, which is real coverage for a Danish audience including business customers. The site is also genuinely fast, which cost me my favourite argument. None of what follows is a theme complaint.

### The finding that justifies a redesign: nothing here was designed

The strongest case against this site is not a defect list, because a defect list argues for a sprint. It is that the interface reads as unmaintained, and visitors extend that judgement to the company. Stanford's web credibility research found visual design to be the most frequently cited reason people gave for trusting or distrusting a site, ahead of the content itself. That matters more here than in most categories: the visitor is deciding whether to let a stranger into their home, or to put poison down near their children.

Easy to assert, easy to dismiss as taste. So I measured it instead. Craft leaves fingerprints.

| Signal | Measured on the homepage | What it means |
|---|---:|---|
| Distinct corner radii in use | **8** | No shared decisions; each component styled alone |
| Distinct text sizes | **9** | Not a type scale, a pile of one-offs |
| Font families on one page | **4** | Webfont wired up wrong, system font leaking in |
| Inline `style` attributes | **136** | Design overridden ad hoc, not owned centrally |
| CSS shipped | **3 KB** | — |
| JavaScript shipped | **547 KB** | The look is plugin defaults, not design |
| Most common body text size, mobile | **12px** | Below comfortable reading size on a phone |

Here is what those eight radii look like as components rather than as a number:

![Four components from the live homepage, each captioned with its own computed border-radius: a search form wrapper at 28.5px, a round button at 50%, the search input at 25px, and a link button at 9px.](/images/billigskadedyr/evidence-radii.png)

The first two tiles are the same search field. A wrapper rounded to 28.5px, with the input inside it rounded to 25px. Three and a half pixels apart, nested one inside the other. Nobody chose that, nobody can see it, and nobody will ever file a bug about it, which is precisely the point: it is the residue of a site assembled rather than designed.

The type tells the same story. Nine sizes, following no ratio:

![Nine text sizes rendered at the sizes measured on the live homepage, from 28px down to 10px, each labelled with how many elements use it. 14px is used by 70 elements and 12px by 34.](/images/billigskadedyr/evidence-type.png)

Nine sizes between 10px and 28px with no consistent step is what happens when each size is picked in isolation. And on mobile the single most common text size on the page is **12px**. The audience is homeowners, frequently middle-aged or older, usually reading on a phone in the room with the problem.

None of this is broken. Nothing on this list would ever be reported by a user or caught by a test. **That is exactly why it needs a redesign rather than a ticket: there is no system to correct, so there is nothing to patch.** Three kilobytes of stylesheet against half a megabyte of JavaScript is not a shop that was designed and then built. It is a shop that was assembled, and visitors read that as a company that assembles things.

### Where the money leaks

Funnel findings, ordered by how close they sit to a completed sale.

**It takes four category screens to reach anything you can buy.** Someone who has just seen a rat in the kitchen has to pass through Rotter og mus, then Rotter, then Rottefælder, then Rottesmækfælder. Only the fourth screen contains products, and only there does a price appear.

![Four screens from the live shop side by side. The first three show only further category tiles with no prices; the fourth finally shows eleven products, all with prices and add-to-basket buttons.](/images/billigskadedyr/evidence-drilldown.png)

Each of those screens asks the visitor to translate their problem into the supplier's vocabulary before it will help. Nobody wakes up wanting a *rottesmækfælde*. They wake up because they heard something in the wall. Four rounds of that, with no prices and nothing buyable in sight, is a long way to walk on faith.

I want to be exact about the credit here, because my first pass on this was wrong. I originally recorded that the category listing showed no prices at all. It does show them: on the fourth screen every one of the eleven products carries a price and an add-to-basket button, and the page has eleven filters and a sort control. The listing is competent. I had measured a category hub and called it a product list. The defect is not the list, it is the four screens standing in front of it, which is a bigger problem and an entirely different fix.

**There is no social proof anywhere in the shop.** Every product in the catalogue has a review count of zero, and there is no review section on the product page. This is the gap that struck me hardest, because the business demonstrably has satisfied customers: it advertises a certification score and a Trustpilot presence in the footer. The proof exists, and it is sitting one level away from the moment it would change a decision.

**The price is formatted for the wrong country.** It renders as `1,199.00kr.` Danish is `1.199,00 kr.` To a Danish reader that reads as a foreign shop or a broken one, at the moment they are deciding whether to trust it with a card number.

**Delivery cost never appears.** Not in the basket, not at checkout. The only delivery information in the flow is a marketing line, *"Hurtig levering: Vi sender fra dag-til-dag."* Fast, yes; free, unclear. On its own this is small and cheap to fix. It earns its place because Baymard's abandonment research puts unexpected cost at the end of checkout at the top of the reasons people leave, and this shop is arranged so the visitor commits at the product page and meets the total at the last step.

**Checkout does not say what it requires.** Eleven visible fields, none carrying a programmatic required marker, and no visual indicator in the usual place. A sighted visitor finds out what is mandatory by submitting and failing; a screen reader user is not told at all. The form also asks for professional pesticide authorisation numbers inline with no explanation, which invites a customer buying a 49 kr ant bait to conclude the shop is not for them.

### The bucket I would fix on Monday, redesign or not

Listing these separately rather than using them to pad the case for a rebuild.

**The consent modal locks the page, and an ad blocker leaves the lock in place.** The dialog is full-viewport and disables scrolling on the document itself, not on the banner. Remove the banner from the page, which is what an ad blocker does, and scrolling stays disabled: no dialog, nothing to dismiss, no way down the page. Those visitors do not file a bug. They leave, and they are counted as a bounce. Cheap to fix, and a sharp symptom, because it is invisible to anyone testing without an ad blocker.

**Placeholder Lorem ipsum was live in production** on the professional site, the two sites disagree on street address and opening hours, and the certification claims link to nothing despite sitting in a public register. Also cheap. Also nobody has looked.

### Structural problems that are neither

- **The shop and the professional service are separate sites with no route between them.** A DIY buyer never learns a technician exists; a service customer never learns they could solve it for 49 kr.
- **Only the flattering score is shown.** The higher certification rating is displayed, the lower and better-known Trustpilot rating is not. Hiding it is the intuitive move and the wrong one, because a visitor who finds it independently now discounts everything else on the page.
- **Article content does not exist without JavaScript.** I found this by accident: pulling twelve articles returned twelve identical pages, because the served HTML contains only navigation. For a business whose guides are its main organic search asset, that is worth knowing.

### One argument I had to drop, and why that matters

I expected page speed to carry this case. The Google and Deloitte work on mobile speed is the most quotable research in the field, and "milliseconds make millions" is a line that ends meetings.

Then I measured.

| | Mobile | Desktop |
|---|---:|---:|
| Largest contentful paint | 156 ms | 296 ms |
| Load complete | 412 ms | 351 ms |
| Cumulative layout shift | 0 | 0 |

The site is fast. The half-megabyte of JavaScript is inelegant and it is not costing this business anything a visitor can feel. So the speed argument is out, and I am leaving in the fact that I wanted it. A statistic that does not describe the site in front of you is not evidence, it is decoration, and a client can tell the difference.

I dropped a second one for the same reason. The widely-quoted figure putting UX return on investment near ten thousand percent traces back to no methodology I can point a client at, and a number I cannot source is a number I cannot defend in a room.

What survives is narrower and holds: design is the most-cited factor in how people judge whether a site can be trusted; companies that treat design as a discipline outgrow those that do not by a wide margin measured in years; roughly seven in ten baskets are abandoned, much of it friction rather than price; and about a third of people leave a brand they actually like after a single bad experience.

### What I would tell the owner

| | Finding | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Consent lock, Lorem ipsum, price format, delivery line | **Ticket.** A day's work. Do it this week |
| 2 | No ratings or reviews anywhere in the catalogue | **Template work.** Weeks, not months |
| 3 | Four category screens before a product, organised by supplier vocabulary | **Redesign.** It is the architecture |
| 4 | No type scale, 8 radii, 12px body text on mobile | **Redesign.** There is no system to fix |

Fix row 1 this week; it costs a day and stops the visible bleeding. But the reason to rebuild is rows 3 and 4. A shop with no shared type scale, eight unrelated corner radii and 12px body text on a phone is telling every visitor something about the company before they have read a word, and no ticket fixes the impression of never having been designed.

## The decisions, and the reasoning

**Lead with the fork, not the catalogue.** The first screen asks the only question that matters: do you want to handle this yourself, or do you want someone to come out? Each option states its starting price and how fast it resolves, so the visitor can self-select before scrolling. This is the two-site problem solved as information architecture rather than as a menu.

**Navigate by pest, not by product.** Rats, ants, wasps, bed bugs, silverfish, wood borers, cockroaches, flies. Each routes to whichever path suits it. Recognition over recall — the visitor picks the thing they have seen, rather than recalling what it is called in a catalogue.

**Replace "call for a price" with a number.** A working estimator returns one concrete figure. An earlier version returned a range, and I killed it: a range makes the reader do arithmetic and still leaves them uncertain, which is most of the original problem restated more politely.

**Ask one question, not five.** The estimator originally asked for pest, area, property type and severity before showing anything. Choosing the pest is the only step that must happen; the rest refine a number that is already on screen. They now sit behind one optional control with sensible defaults. On desktop that panel opens as the section scrolls into view, so the controls are present without greeting the visitor as a wall of form fields.

**Make the answer respond to the question.** The "what's included" list used to state the same four promises no matter what you picked. It now derives from the selections: rats list rodent-proofing and bait stations, wasps list nest removal, a commercial property swaps in audit documentation. A list that never changes is decoration; a list that changes is feedback, and feedback is what tells someone their input registered.

**Use one control, not three buttons.** The severity and property choices became segmented controls. Three separated pills read as three independent actions; a single shared outline says "pick one of these", which is what the choice actually is. The form communicates its own constraint.

**Let people undo.** A reset appears as soon as anything is adjusted. Cheap, and it removes the small fear that fiddling will strand you somewhere.

**Treat mobile as a different design problem, not a narrower one.** The comparison table became a horizontal scroller on a phone, which defeats a comparison table — you cannot compare columns you cannot see at once. It became one card per package instead, folded away by default. A contact bar is pinned within thumb reach, because on a page this long the header call-to-action is off screen for most of the visit.

**Show the weaker review score.** Both ratings are displayed with their real counts and a link out. The concept says, in Danish, that it shows the rating as it is even when it is not five stars. That is a design decision, not a copy decision: it is the section deciding to be checkable.

## Holding the concept to the same standard

An audit that only points outward is a sales document. So I ran the same
measurements over my own page, and it failed too.

**Colour.** Lime is the concept's signature, and I had been using it as a text
colour. On white it is 1.79:1; the darker shade is 2.55:1. Both fail. It showed
up in the comparison table headers, the step numerals, the pull-quote mark, the
tick marks and the disclosure chevrons.

| | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Text failing WCAG AA | 8 elements | **0** |
| Lime on white | 1.79:1 | **6.2:1** |
| Lime on cream | 1.65:1 | **5.7:1** |

The fix was one token: the same hue taken dark enough to read, used on light
sections only. Dark sections keep the bright lime, which already clears 10:1 on
ink. One of my swaps was wrong and I caught it on the re-run: an eyebrow that
looked like it sat on a white section actually sat on a dark panel inside it, so
darkening made it worse. *Light section* and *light background* are not the same
claim.

**Selection.** Dragging across the page highlighted everything: button labels,
badges, the step numbers, the currency suffixes around a price. The rule I
settled on is one question, do you read this or operate it, and the numbers moved
from 118 questionable selections to 29, all of which are either body copy or the
contact details I deliberately left copyable.

My first attempt at that rule failed silently, and the reason is the same lesson
as a slider bug earlier in the project: the component library's links carry a
class, a class outranks a bare element selector, and my rule lost the cascade
without any error to tell me. The second attempt made selection opt-in instead
of trying to enumerate exceptions.

**Identity.** The concept had been calling the business "SkadedyrPro" in the
header, the footer and the tab title. That is a name I invented for a company
that already has one, which is not a redesign of their site so much as a
redesign of somebody else's. The mark now comes from their own logo with the
wordmark cropped away, it doubles as the favicon the page never had, and it
answers the browser's colour scheme so it does not vanish on a light tab strip.

The through-line, and the reason this section exists at all: the audit only has
authority if the thing making it can survive being pointed at itself.

## What the review loop taught me about myself

### Where I am fast

**Catching visual defects.** A twelve-pixel gap at the end of a slider. One icon in a set of eight carrying visibly less weight than its neighbours. A checkmark sitting two pixels below its line of text. One item in a row of four wrapping differently from the rest. None of these were caught by any automated check, and several survived multiple passes by an agent looking straight at them.

This is the part of design work that does not survive delegation, and it is worth knowing that mine is quick.

**Spotting copy that contradicts the page.** A comparison section claimed visitors could not learn the price without calling — on a page with a working price calculator two sections above it. The sentence read fluently, which is exactly why it was dangerous. The same instinct caught a hero promising a free inspection while every package listed that inspection as an included item, and a table row that quoted a response time contradicting the promise made elsewhere on the same screen.

**Language and market authority.** I rejected "priskalkulator" because Danes say "prisberegner". I rejected em dashes because Danish does not use them. These are not stylistic preferences; they are the kind of correctness that has to come from inside the language and market.

### Where I was slower than I should have been

**I reviewed output instead of writing acceptance criteria.** Nearly every correction I made could have been a line in the brief: one price, never a range. Danish, no em dashes. Email before phone. 44px minimum, verified by script rather than by eye. I supplied each of these reactively, after seeing it violated, one at a time. Roughly two thirds of thirty review rounds were standards I already held and had never written down.

**I let scope grow without ever freezing anything.** Palette, then icons, then copy, then a component library migration, then a mobile pass, then heuristics, then archiving the old site. Every request was reasonable. Together they meant nothing was ever finished, and several things I had signed off were reopened because a later requirement contradicted them.

**I accepted a confident summary as a status report.** The first build was reported complete, with accessibility and 44px touch targets among its claims. Both were partly true and neither had been verified. The mobile audit later found nine violations that had been present since day one. I did not ask how it knew until much later, and that is on me.

### The habit I actually changed

Late in the build the agent stopped and reported that a credential was dead, asking me to issue a new one. I did not take that at face value. I tested it myself, found it working, and told the agent it was wrong.

I was half right. The credential was fine; the real cause was that its store held several entries and the wrong one was being matched first. My diagnosis was better than the agent's and still not correct.

What stuck was the shape of the mistake: **the error you are shown describes a symptom, and the symptom is rarely where the fault lives.** I then watched myself make that exact error three more times during the review — treating a layout problem as a colour problem for three rounds, adjusting icon sizes by eye when the underlying difference was measurable, and fixing a text-clipping bug only at the screen width where it had been reported rather than everywhere the cause applied.

The correction is simple and I now apply it as a rule: **if I have adjusted the same thing twice without it converging, stop and measure.** That turned two multi-round loops into single passes.

The audit gave me a second version of the same lesson, which is why I am confident it is a real habit and not a story I tell. I had a script counting prices on what I believed was a category listing. It returned zero, which fitted my thesis neatly, and I wrote it up. It was only when I looked at the screenshot the script had taken, in order to put it in this case study, that I saw the page was a grid of subcategories rather than products. **Measuring is not enough if you never look at what you measured.** The corrected finding turned out to be worse for the client than the wrong one, but that was luck, not method.

I did it a third time in the same audit, and this one is the sharpest of the three because I published it before it was caught. The shop carries e-mærket, the Danish certification mark, through a third-party widget. I probed for it, found three empty containers, waited several seconds, found them still empty, and wrote up that the site's strongest trust signal renders nothing. It was a satisfying finding and it was false. The widget renders through a custom element with a shadow root, and it takes longer to populate than I had allowed. Waiting fourteen seconds instead of six shows it working perfectly.

The error was not the short wait. It was that "nothing rendered" and "nothing rendered *yet*" produce identical evidence, and I did not design the check to tell them apart. A negative finding needs a stronger standard of proof than a positive one, because absence is exactly what a not-yet-finished page also looks like. I now treat any finding of the form "X is missing" as unproven until I have either seen X somewhere else or given it a deliberately unreasonable amount of time to appear.

## What I changed in how I work

- **Acceptance criteria go in the brief, not the review.** Language rules, pricing display, contact hierarchy, touch-target minimums, and how each will be verified.
- **Measure before adjusting anything a second time.**
- **Archive the before-state before touching anything,** to a public web archive and as my own captures. It makes the comparison citable instead of a claim.
- **Treat a collaborator's summary as a claim, not evidence.** "Verified" means I can point at what did the verifying.

## Open items I would not hide from the client

- The review quotes in the concept came from an early pass and I have not re-checked them against the live source. The aggregate scores are real and linked; the quotes are flagged in the source and should be re-collected or removed before this is shown to the business. Inventing plausible reviewer names would have filled the section and been worth nothing.
- The professional prices are market estimates from other Danish firms, labelled as indicative throughout, because this business publishes none of its own. The DIY prices are genuinely theirs.
- The weakest reviews on the live business concern order fulfilment and support, not design. No redesign fixes that, and it should be fixed first.
