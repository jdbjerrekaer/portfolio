## What this case study is about

The redesign is the occasion. The subject is how I work.

An autonomous agent running on my own hardware built the entire first version of this concept while I was away from the keyboard. When I came back, I reviewed it the way I would review a junior designer's work, and that review ran about thirty rounds across a day.

That gap is the interesting part. A capable agent, given a researched brief, produced something coherent that was still wrong in roughly thirty specific ways — and almost none of those ways were things the brief had mentioned. They were things I only knew when I saw them. Working out *why* I only knew them then, and not before, is what this piece is about.

## The client situation

A Danish pest-control business runs two separate websites. One is a webshop selling DIY products: traps, baits, sprays. The other sells professional treatment, where a technician comes to your house.

A visitor has to already know which of those they need before they land on either. Neither site says: same company, two options, here is how to tell which one you are.

## What was actually wrong with the existing site

I audited the live site before designing anything, and archived it publicly so the comparison would be against a fixed state rather than my memory of it. What I found sorts into three groups.

### Problems that block the sale

**There are no prices for the professional service. Anywhere.** Every service page ends at "ring og få et uforpligtende tilbud" — call for a quote. In a category where people are anxious, embarrassed, and want to know if this is a 500 kr or a 5,000 kr problem before they speak to anyone, that is the single largest conversion leak on the site. Baymard's work on price transparency is unambiguous here: hidden pricing does not defer the decision, it ends it.

**Products are organised by product type, not by problem.** The navigation offers categories like traps and bait stations. Nobody wakes up wanting a bait station. They wake up because they heard something in the wall. The taxonomy asks the visitor to translate their problem into the vocabulary of a supplier before they can find anything.

**Nothing connects the shop to the service.** A DIY buyer never learns the shop is curated by a professional who could come out. A service customer never sees that they might solve it themselves for 49 kr. Each site quietly withholds the other's option.

### Problems that break trust

**Placeholder text in production.** The professional site had Lorem ipsum live on it. Nothing else on this list costs as much credibility per pixel.

**The two sites contradict each other on facts.** Different street addresses. Different opening hours. If a visitor checks both — and an anxious one will — the business looks like it does not know its own details.

**Professional authorisation is asserted but not checkable.** The site claims the relevant Danish certifications. Those sit in a public register, and nothing links to it. A claim you cannot verify does less work than a claim you can.

**The review picture is uneven.** A 4.6 out of 5 sits prominently; a 3.8 out of 5 from a larger, better-known platform is not shown. Hiding the weaker score is the intuitive move and the wrong one, because the reader who finds it independently now distrusts everything else on the page.

### Problems in the interface itself

I measured these rather than eyeballing them, at a desktop and a phone width:

- **The layout overflows the screen.** Two elements push past the viewport on desktop, four on mobile. Horizontal scrolling on a phone is the kind of defect people experience as "this site feels broken" without being able to name it.
- **Tap targets are too small to hit reliably.** 32 controls under the 44px minimum on the mobile homepage, 36 on the article index. On a site whose primary action is contacting a tradesperson from a phone, that is a real barrier, not a technicality.
- **The article index is over 10,000 pixels tall on mobile** and unusually heavy, mostly unresized imagery.
- **The article content is invisible to anything that does not run JavaScript.** I discovered this by accident: when I pulled twelve articles to read them, all twelve came back identical, because the served page contains only the navigation. For a business whose articles are its main organic search asset, that is worth knowing.

That last one is a good example of why I archive and probe a site rather than just screenshotting it. The problem was invisible from the browser and obvious the moment I looked at what the server actually sends.

## The decisions, and the reasoning

**Lead with the fork, not the catalogue.** The first screen asks the only question that matters: do you want to handle this yourself, or do you want someone to come out? Each option states its starting price and how fast it resolves, so the visitor can self-select before scrolling. This is the two-site problem solved as information architecture rather than as a menu.

**Navigate by pest, not by product.** Rats, ants, wasps, bed bugs, silverfish, wood borers, cockroaches, flies. Each routes to whichever path suits it. Recognition over recall — the visitor picks the thing they have seen, rather than recalling what it is called in a catalogue.

**Replace "call for a price" with a number.** A working estimator returns one concrete figure. An earlier version returned a range, and I killed it: a range makes the reader do arithmetic and still leaves them uncertain, which is most of the original problem restated more politely.

**Ask one question, not five.** The estimator originally asked for pest, area, property type and severity before showing anything. Choosing the pest is the only step that must happen; the rest refine a number that is already on screen. They now sit behind one optional control with sensible defaults. On desktop that panel opens as the section scrolls into view, so the controls are present without greeting the visitor as a wall of form fields.

**Make the answer respond to the question.** The "what's included" list used to state the same four promises no matter what you picked. It now derives from the selections: rats list rodent-proofing and bait stations, wasps list nest removal, a commercial property swaps in audit documentation. A list that never changes is decoration; a list that changes is feedback, and feedback is what tells someone their input registered.

**Use one control, not three buttons.** The severity and property choices became segmented controls. Three separated pills read as three independent actions; a single shared outline says "pick one of these", which is what the choice actually is. The form communicates its own constraint.

**Let people undo.** A reset appears as soon as anything is adjusted. Cheap, and it removes the small fear that fiddling will strand you somewhere.

**Treat mobile as a different design problem, not a narrower one.** The comparison table became a horizontal scroller on a phone, which defeats a comparison table — you cannot compare columns you cannot see at once. It became one card per package instead, folded away by default. A contact bar is pinned within thumb reach, because on a page this long the header call-to-action is off screen for most of the visit.

**Show the weaker review score.** Both ratings are displayed with their real counts and a link out. The concept says, in Danish, that it shows the rating as it is even when it is not five stars. That is a design decision, not a copy decision: it is the section deciding to be checkable.

## What the review loop taught me about myself

### Where I am fast

**Catching visual defects.** A twelve-pixel gap at the end of a slider. One icon in a set of eight carrying visibly less weight than its neighbours. A checkmark sitting two pixels below its line of text. One item in a row of four wrapping differently from the rest. None of these were caught by any automated check, and several survived multiple passes by an agent looking straight at them.

This is the part of design work that does not survive delegation, and it is worth knowing that mine is quick.

**Spotting copy that contradicts the page.** A comparison section claimed visitors could not learn the price without calling — on a page with a working price calculator two sections above it. The sentence read fluently, which is exactly why it was dangerous. The same instinct caught a hero promising a free inspection while every package listed that inspection as an included item, and a table row that quoted a response time contradicting the promise made elsewhere on the same screen.

**Language and market authority.** I rejected "priskalkulator" because Danes say "prisberegner". I rejected em dashes because Danish does not use them. These are not stylistic preferences; they are the kind of correctness that has to come from inside the language and market.

### Where I was slower than I should have been

**I reviewed output instead of writing acceptance criteria.** Nearly every correction I made could have been a line in the brief: one price, never a range. Danish, no em dashes. Email before phone. 44px minimum, verified by script rather than by eye. I supplied each of these reactively, after seeing it violated, one at a time. Roughly two thirds of thirty review rounds were standards I already held and had never written down.

**I let scope grow without ever freezing anything.** Palette, then icons, then copy, then a component library migration, then a mobile pass, then heuristics, then archiving the old site. Every request was reasonable. Together they meant nothing was ever finished, and several things I had signed off were reopened because a later requirement contradicted them.

**I accepted a confident summary as a status report.** The first build was reported complete, with accessibility and 44px touch targets among its claims. Both were partly true and neither had been verified. The mobile audit later found nine violations that had been present since day one. I did not ask how it knew until much later, and that is on me.

### The habit I actually changed

Late in the build the agent stopped and reported that a credential was dead, asking me to issue a new one. I did not take that at face value. I tested it myself, found it working, and told the agent it was wrong.

I was half right. The credential was fine; the real cause was that its store held several entries and the wrong one was being matched first. My diagnosis was better than the agent's and still not correct.

What stuck was the shape of the mistake: **the error you are shown describes a symptom, and the symptom is rarely where the fault lives.** I then watched myself make that exact error three more times during the review — treating a layout problem as a colour problem for three rounds, adjusting icon sizes by eye when the underlying difference was measurable, and fixing a text-clipping bug only at the screen width where it had been reported rather than everywhere the cause applied.

The correction is simple and I now apply it as a rule: **if I have adjusted the same thing twice without it converging, stop and measure.** That turned two multi-round loops into single passes.

The last two rows go the wrong way and I am keeping them visible. The concept is heavier, largely from the component library, and much longer — partly because a landing page is a different genre from a shop grid, and partly because I chose to add thirteen article extracts for search coverage, which cost real height. A before/after table where every row flatters the redesign is a marketing document, and anyone who has shipped work knows it.

## What I changed in how I work

- **Acceptance criteria go in the brief, not the review.** Language rules, pricing display, contact hierarchy, touch-target minimums, and how each will be verified.
- **Measure before adjusting anything a second time.**
- **Archive the before-state before touching anything,** to a public web archive and as my own captures. It makes the comparison citable instead of a claim.
- **Treat a collaborator's summary as a claim, not evidence.** "Verified" means I can point at what did the verifying.

## Open items I would not hide from the client

- The review quotes in the concept came from an early pass and I have not re-checked them against the live source. The aggregate scores are real and linked; the quotes are flagged in the source and should be re-collected or removed before this is shown to the business. Inventing plausible reviewer names would have filled the section and been worth nothing.
- The professional prices are market estimates from other Danish firms, labelled as indicative throughout, because this business publishes none of its own. The DIY prices are genuinely theirs.
- The weakest reviews on the live business concern order fulfilment and support, not design. No redesign fixes that, and it should be fixed first.
