## Why this case study is about process

Most portfolio pieces show the artefact and imply the thinking. This one inverts it, because the artefact is the least interesting output.

The subject is a Danish pest-control business running two properties: a webshop selling DIY products, and a separate service site selling professional treatment. The redesign question is genuinely interesting (one entry point, two audiences), but I have written that kind of analysis before. What I had not done before is run a project where an autonomous agent produced the entire first version while I was away from the keyboard, and then spend a working day reviewing it as a design director rather than an author.

That produced something more useful than a landing page: a fairly precise map of where my design judgement is fast and trustworthy, where it is slow, and where it was simply absent until a defect made it visible.

## How the work was actually structured

**Phase one, autonomous.** A long-running agent on my own hardware held the brief. My instruction before it started was deliberately not "build it" but:

> "Maybe do a double take on the plan and research like a second pass to see if we missed anything in the planning or research, so when you finish this task you don't start from scratch."

That was the right instinct and I want to keep it. Re-reading a plan before executing it is cheap; discovering a missing constraint after a build is not. The second pass caught real things: the two domains disagree on address and opening hours, the professional site had placeholder text in production, and the authorisation claims were not verifiable from a public register.

It returned a complete, deployed React application. Two-path router, pest-first navigation, a working price estimator, real review scores, Danish throughout, an audit document and a handover document listing open questions for the owner.

**Phase two, review.** I opened it and started looking. That phase ran roughly thirty rounds of feedback across a day.

The gap between those two phases is the finding. A capable agent given a good brief produced something coherent that was still wrong in about thirty specific ways, and almost none of those ways were things the brief had mentioned. They were things I only knew when I saw them.

## What the review loop revealed about my judgement

### Where I am fast and reliable

**Visual defect detection, at a level I had not consciously credited myself with.** I caught a twelve-pixel gap at the left end of a slider. I caught that one icon in a set of eight carried visibly less ink than the others. I caught that a badge sat two pixels below its text baseline, and that one item in a four-item row wrapped to one line while its neighbours took two. None of these were flagged by any automated check; several survived multiple rounds of an agent looking directly at them.

This is worth naming because it is the part of design work that does not survive delegation. An agent can execute a layout. Noticing that the layout is subtly wrong is a different faculty, and mine is quick.

**Catching factual contradictions in copy.** The comparison section claimed a visitor could not find out the price without calling. The page it sat on had a working price calculator two sections above. The copy read fluently, which is exactly why it was dangerous. I flagged it in seconds because I was reading the page as a visitor rather than as its author.

The same instinct caught "gratis inspektion" in the hero while every package listed the inspection as an included line item, and a comparison table row labelled "responstid: 1-2 dage" on a page that promised a response within 24 hours and arrival in 1-2 days. Fluent, confident, and wrong.

**Domain and language authority.** I rejected "priskalkulator" because Danes say "prisberegner". I rejected em dashes because Danish does not use them. I rejected price ranges because a range makes the reader do arithmetic and still leaves them uncertain. These are not preferences; they are the kind of correctness that only someone inside the language and market can supply, and no amount of model capability substitutes for it.

### Where I was slower than I should have been

**I reviewed output instead of setting constraints.** Almost every correction I made could have been a line in the brief. "Single price, never a range." "Danish, no em dashes." "Email before phone everywhere." "44px minimum touch targets, verified by script, not by eye." I supplied each of these reactively, after seeing it violated, one at a time, across thirty rounds.

That is an expensive way to hold a standard. It also means the standard only gets applied where I happened to look. The mobile pass found nine tap targets under 44px that had been there since the first build, in a project whose original handover notes claimed 44px touch targets as a completed requirement.

**I let scope grow without freezing anything.** The work expanded continuously: palette, then icons, then copy, then a component-library migration, then a mobile overhaul, then two heuristic frameworks, then a full archive of the old site. Each request was reasonable. Together they meant nothing was ever finished, and several things I had signed off on were later reopened because a new requirement contradicted them.

**I accepted a confident summary as a status report.** The agent reported the first build as complete with "WCAG AA, 44px touch targets" among its claims. Both were partly true and neither had been verified. I did not ask how it knew until much later. That is on me: I treated a well-written summary as evidence.

## The one incident that changed how I work

Late in the autonomous phase, the agent stopped and reported that the credential it needed was dead, and asked me to issue a new one.

I did not take that at face value. I tested the credential myself: it returned a valid identity with the right permissions. I told the agent it was wrong, and explained the likely mechanism — a permission-scoped credential returns "not found", not "forbidden", for a resource it cannot see, so reading a 404 as an authentication failure would produce exactly that false conclusion.

I was half right. When I looked properly, the real cause was that the credential store held three entries and the tool was matching the first one, which was stale. The credential was fine. The lookup order was not.

Two things came out of that.

The first is a working principle I now apply everywhere: **an error message describes a symptom, and the symptom is usually not the layer where the fault lives.** A 404 read as an auth failure, a stale first line read as a dead credential — same shape of mistake.

The second is that I watched myself make the identical class of error repeatedly during the review phase, which is what makes it a genuine lesson rather than a complaint about a tool:

- A slider kept rendering a blue segment. Three separate fixes treated it as a colour problem. It was not: the component library draws the filled portion as a twelve-pixel left border on the track. Measuring it settled it immediately — the track began at x=96 and the fill at x=108. Those twelve pixels were the artefact. Recolouring removed the colour and left the gap. Setting the width to zero fixed it.
- Icons in a set looked mismatched. Nudging sizes by eye never converged, because the densest glyph carried two and a half times the ink of the sparsest, so the dense one needed to shrink while the sparse one grew. Measuring alpha coverage produced the answer in one pass.
- Text was clipping on mobile. I fixed the mobile case and stopped. The same class was clipping at every desktop width too, and I had to be told twice before I checked across breakpoints instead of only where the complaint came from.

The pattern is the same in all four: **acting on the first plausible explanation, when a measurement was available and cheap.**

## The design decisions, and why

With that lens, the substantive decisions were these.

**Progressive disclosure on the estimator.** The first version asked five questions before showing anything. Choosing the pest is the only step that must happen; area, property type and severity refine a number that is already on screen. They now sit behind one control with sensible defaults. On desktop the panel opens as the section scrolls into view — and the trigger point had to be moved twice, because firing it too early meant the panel had finished opening before the visitor's eye arrived, which defeats the entire signal.

**One number, not a range.** Baymard's comparison-UX work is blunt about hidden and vague pricing being a conversion leak. A range technically discloses more and communicates less.

**A segmented control instead of three separate buttons.** Three detached pills read as three independent actions. A shared outline says "pick one of these", which is what the choice actually is. This is Norman's constraints and mapping doing real work rather than being cited decoratively.

**Feedback tied to input.** The "what's included" list used to state the same four promises regardless of input. It now derives from the selections, so choosing rats lists rodent-proofing and bait stations, and choosing a commercial property swaps the documentation line to audit documentation. Shneiderman's third rule is informative feedback; a list that never changes is not feedback, it is decoration.

**Reversal.** A reset control appears once any refinement is touched. Shneiderman's sixth rule, and about four lines of code.

**Mobile as a separate design problem.** The comparison table was a 560px-wide horizontal scroller on a 390px screen: you cannot compare columns you cannot see simultaneously. It became one card per tier, folded away by default. A contact bar is pinned to the bottom, because on a page this long the header CTA is off screen for most of a visit.

**Selection behaviour.** Controls disable text selection; content does not. Dragging the slider used to smear a highlight across the labels beside it. But phone numbers, email addresses, the price and the address stay selectable, because those are the things people actually copy.

## What the numbers say, including where the concept is worse

Measured with the same scripted audit against both the live site and the concept, at 1440 and 390.

| | Live site | Concept |
|---|---|---|
| Elements overflowing the viewport (desktop / mobile) | 2 / 4 | **0 / 0** |
| Tap targets under 44px, mobile | 32 | **1** |
| Tap targets under 44px, article index mobile | 36 | — |
| HTTP requests | 47–61 | **13** |
| Transferred weight | ~750 KB | 1.27 MB |
| Page height, mobile | 3,200px | 13,621px |

The last two rows go the wrong way. The component library and its motion dependency account for most of the weight, and the length is partly genre (a long landing page against a shop grid) and partly mine — adding thirteen article extracts for search coverage cost real height. I am keeping both rows visible. A before/after table where every row favours the redesign is a marketing document, and anyone who has shipped software knows it.

## What I changed, concretely

- **Acceptance criteria go in the brief, not in the review.** Language rules, pricing display, contact hierarchy, touch-target minimums and the verification method are now stated before a build starts. Roughly two thirds of my thirty rounds were criteria I held but had not written down.
- **Measure before adjusting anything twice.** If I have corrected the same visual property twice without converging, I stop and measure it. Coverage, bounding boxes, computed styles. This turned two multi-round loops into single passes.
- **Diagnose the layer, not the symptom.** The most valuable thing I learned technically was why my overrides kept losing: the utility framework emits into a CSS layer and the component library does not, and unlayered rules beat layered ones regardless of specificity. Once that was understood, four unrelated bugs collapsed into one cause.
- **Capture the "before" before touching anything.** I archived the live site to a public web archive and took my own full-page captures at both viewports first. It makes the comparison honest and citable rather than a claim.
- **Treat agent self-reports as claims, not evidence.** "Verified" now means I can point at the command that verified it.

## Honest open items

- The review quotes on the concept were carried over from an early pass and I have not re-verified them against the live source. The aggregate scores are real and linked; the individual quotes are flagged in the source and should be re-scraped or cut before this is shown to the business. Inventing plausible reviewer names and star ratings would have filled the section and been worthless.
- The professional prices are market estimates from other Danish firms, labelled as indicative throughout, because this business publishes no professional prices at all. The DIY figures are genuinely theirs, taken from their own shop data.
- The one-star reviews on the live business are about order fulfilment and support. No redesign fixes that, and it should be fixed first.
