## Recruiter takeaway

This is a scope-and-clarity story with numbers attached, not a visual refresh.

The business behind billigskadedyr.dk runs two properties that tell two stories: a WooCommerce webshop selling DIY pest-control products, and a separate service site selling professional treatment. A visitor has to already know which story they need before they land. The concept unifies them into one entry point, lets the user pick their path in the first screen, and replaces "kontakt os for pris" with an estimator that returns one concrete number.

What this proves:

- I audit a live product before touching a pixel, and I measure the result against it afterwards rather than asserting an improvement
- I make the IA decision first: two paths (DIY vs. professional) instead of forcing one funnel on two audiences
- I build the interface I design: a React/TypeScript/Tailwind app on HeroUI that compiles, deploys, and is verified at 390 and 1440 with a scripted audit
- I am specific about provenance, including where my data is *not* the client's

## The problem

**Two sites, one customer, zero handoff.**

The webshop sells traps, baits and professional-grade products. The service site sells the labour. Neither says: here is the same company, here are your two options, here is how to tell which one you need.

- A DIY buyer never learns the shop is curated by a professional
- A service customer never sees the products they could buy instead
- Pricing is opaque on the service side, which is the single biggest conversion leak in pest control

## Measuring the "before" first

I archived the live site before designing anything, so the comparison would be against a fixed, citable state rather than my memory of it. Two records: a [Wayback capture](https://web.archive.org/web/20260829200352/https://billigskadedyr.dk/) as a timestamped third-party record, and my own full-page captures at 1440 and 390 for pixel control.

A detail worth recording: the article pages are JS-rendered, and their served HTML contains only the navigation. My first scrape returned twelve byte-identical "articles" that were all nav markup. I caught it by checking how many distinct bodies came back (one) before using any of it. The real content comes from the site's own WordPress REST API.

## Designing the entry point

**The first job was IA, not visual.**

The palette is near-black green with an acid-lime accent. That pairing matters: a mid-tone green reads garden centre, while near-black plus lime reads treatment and chemistry, which is the category this business is actually in.

1. **Two-path router.** "Klar det selv" vs. "Få en professionel ud", each stating its price starting point and time to result, so the user self-selects before scrolling.
2. **Pest-first navigation.** A grid of the common pests routing each to the correct path. People think in pests, not product categories.
3. **Delivery time up front.** Speed is the promise the original site leads with, so it leads here too.

## The estimator

**The centrepiece is a working calculator, and it returns one number.**

An early version showed a range. A range makes the visitor do the arithmetic and still leaves them unsure, so the output is a single figure with the inputs visible. The breakdown underneath is derived from the selections rather than fixed: choosing rats lists *sikring af adgangsveje og opsætning af foderstationer*, wasps lists nest removal, and picking Erhverv swaps the documentation line to audit documentation.

Progressive disclosure does the rest of the work. Choosing the pest is the only visible step; area, property type and severity sit behind one control with sensible defaults, and on desktop that panel opens as the section scrolls into view so the controls are present without being a wall on arrival.

**On provenance, precisely:**

- **DIY prices are real and theirs.** Scraped from the shop's own WooCommerce Store API, 167 products, so the "from" figures are the client's actual prices.
- **Professional prices are market estimates, and the UI says so.** The business publishes no professional call-out prices at all, so there was nothing of theirs to quote. Those figures come from other Danish firms' public price pages and are labelled *vejledende* throughout. Presenting them as the client's numbers would have been the easy lie.

## Before and after, measured

Captured with the same scripted audit against both sites, desktop 1440 and mobile 390.

| | Live site | Concept |
|---|---|---|
| Elements overflowing the viewport (desktop / mobile) | 2 / 4 | **0 / 0** |
| Tap targets under 44px, mobile | 32 | **1** |
| Tap targets under 44px, article index mobile | 36 | n/a |
| HTTP requests | 47–61 | **13** |

The wins that matter are the overflow and the tap targets. Horizontal overflow on a phone is the kind of defect people feel as "this site is broken" without being able to name it, and 32 sub-44px targets on a page whose primary action is contacting a tradesperson is a real barrier.

**Where the concept is worse, and why I am leaving it in the table:**

- **It is heavier.** 1.27 MB against roughly 750 KB. The component library plus its motion dependency accounts for most of it. Fewer, larger requests instead of many small ones is a defensible trade, but it is a trade, not a free win.
- **It is longer.** 13,621px on mobile against 3,200px. Partly genre — a long-form landing page against a shop grid — and partly mine: adding thirteen article extracts for search coverage cost real height. The comparison-matrix and refinement panels are folded away on mobile for exactly this reason.

## Building on someone else's component library

Migrating to HeroUI produced one lesson worth writing down, because it caused four separate visual bugs before I understood it.

Tailwind v4 emits its utilities inside `@layer utilities`; the library's stylesheet is unlayered. **Unlayered rules beat layered ones regardless of specificity**, so a utility class on the JSX can never override a library rule. That is why the nav went dark-on-dark, the package tier names vanished, and the slider kept its default blue: I was writing overrides in a layer that could not win.

The slider took three attempts because I kept treating it as a colour problem. The library draws the filled end as a **12px left border on the track**, not as part of the fill. Measuring it settled it in one pass: track at x=96, fill at x=108. Those 12px were the artifact. Recolouring the border removed the colour and left the gap; setting its width to zero fixed it.

The same discipline fixed the icons. The pest glyphs looked mismatched, and nudging sizes by eye never converged. Measuring the alpha coverage of each PNG showed why: the cockroach carries 2.5× the ink of the beetle at identical box size, so the dense one needed scaling *down* while the sparse one scaled up.

## What I would do next

**Three gaps, in order of impact.**

- The one-star reviews are about order fulfilment and support, not design. No redesign fixes that, and it should be fixed operationally first.
- The review quotes in the concept are carried over from an earlier pass and I have not re-verified them against the live Trustpilot page. The aggregate scores are real and linked; the individual quotes carry a warning in the source and should be re-scraped or cut before this goes anywhere public. Inventing reviewer names and star counts to fill the section would have looked better and been worthless.
- The two domains disagree on address and opening hours. That should be reconciled before a unified design is proposed to the owner.
