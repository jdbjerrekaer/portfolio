*University work. It is here because the method is still how I work.*

## The result

Walk from the kitchen to the living room and the music follows you, mid-song, without touching a phone. We measured that handover at 0.46 seconds on average. The off-the-shelf alternative took between 30 seconds and 4 minutes to do the same thing.

This was our bachelor's thesis at Aarhus University in 2021, with three of us on it. The question we set out to answer: can music streaming work in a modern multi-person smart home with no pauses, no interrupted songs, and instant speaker handover as people move between rooms?

## My role

The idea was mine and I led the project — framing the question, planning the work, splitting it between us, and keeping a six-month thesis on track. I worked across most of the build rather than owning one slice of it, which meant I was also the one holding the argument together: what we were claiming, and what we had actually proven.

## What we built

A working system, not a mockup. One Raspberry Pi 4 per room, Bluetooth signal strength for indoor positioning, a tracking service we wrote in Python 3, and Spotify Connect through Raspotify for playback. Spotify Group Session handled multiple listeners in the same room.

## We measured it instead of asserting it worked

- **2,077 timed handover samples.** Mean 0.46 s, SD 0.58, range 0.1 to 10.8 s. Most switches land near half a second.
- **Benchmarked against the alternative.** Home Assistant took 30 seconds to 4 minutes for the same handover on the same setup.
- **A 65,000-entry signal study** to derive the room-detection thresholds, roughly 31 minutes of data per position across two rooms and multiple positions.
- **A deliberately hard test environment.** A 63 m² apartment with thin walls, chosen because signal bleed between rooms made the problem harder, not easier. Doors closed for every cross-room measurement, both devices rebooted before every run.

## The technical choices were product choices

**Extra sensors rejected on product grounds, not technical ones.** PIR, camera, laser and ultrasonic were all evaluated and dropped. Each would have improved accuracy, and each needed visible wiring that made the system clunky to install and ugly in a home. A usability call that overrode the technically better option.

**Opt-in over opt-out for shared playback.** Auto-joining people to a group session would have been more automated. It would also mean walking into a room hijacks what someone else is listening to. We chose user control over automation.

**Bluetooth classic over BLE and Wi-Fi.** We tried BLE first and dropped it on hardware availability inside the timebox. Bluetooth classic measured more precise than Wi-Fi-only positioning in our own testing. We wrote down the cost too: modern phones scramble their address for unpaired devices, so a user has to pair before the system can see them.

**Build versus buy, decided on two stated axes:** time investment and modifiability. We built the tracking ourselves because we needed control over it, and handed playback and multi-user handling to Spotify because we did not.

## What we would do next

One Raspberry Pi per room caps how precisely you can place someone, and false positives between adjacent rooms were hard to eliminate. There was no interface: registering a device meant editing the Python source, so nobody outside the team could set it up.

Both are named in the thesis as the next things to solve — better positioning, and a setup flow built for someone who is not a programmer.
